[Robinhood](/review/robinhood) reports more than **25 million** funded customers, which makes it one of the largest retail brokerages ever built. It built that base in roughly a decade by removing the two things that kept ordinary people out of the market: a commission on every trade and a minimum balance to open an account.

*So why do so many of its users end up looking for something else?*

Because the app that is best at getting you started is not the one you want three years later. Once you start caring about execution quality, or want a retirement account and a bond allocation rather than a watchlist, the limits of a deliberately simple product show. None of that makes Robinhood a bad broker. It makes it a specific one.

Here is the honest breakdown.

## The short answer

For community and copy trading, or for investing from outside the United States, [eToro](/review/etoro) is the natural switch. For active trading, [Webull](/review/webull) is the strongest free option. If order routing is what bothers you, [Public](/review/public) is built around that objection. If you would rather automate a long-term portfolio than watch it, compare [M1 Finance](/review/m1-finance) and [Wealthfront](/review/wealthfront). In the UK or the EU, [Trading 212](/review/trading-212) is the closest equivalent.

## Why people look for an alternative

The most common objection is **payment for order flow**. Robinhood routes your orders to market makers who pay for the privilege, and that payment is the main reason the trade is **commission-free**. Whether it costs you anything is contested: routing can deliver price improvement, and it can also mean your order was never shopped as aggressively as it might have been. The SEC fined Robinhood **$65 million** in 2020 over how it described its execution quality, which is why the subject persists.

The second objection is tooling. Research is thin, screening is basic, and the charting is designed to be glanced at rather than worked in. Traders who want depth usually pair the app with something else, which is largely why our [TradingView review](/blog/tradingview-review-2026) is among the most read pieces on this site.

The third is product range. There are no mutual funds, and the fixed income and international options are narrow. Robinhood added retirement accounts with a contribution match in 2023, but the account menu is still short next to a full-service broker.

Then there is reliability. The March 2020 outages and the January 2021 decision to restrict buying in several heavily shorted stocks cost the firm a great deal of trust, and FINRA fined it roughly **$70 million** in 2021. The platform is more robust now. Some people do not want to find out the hard way.

## eToro - best for social and copy trading

eToro's distinguishing feature is **copy trading**: you mirror another investor's positions automatically, with your own capital, at a size you choose. Combined with public portfolios and discussion threads, it turns investing into a social feed, which some find instructive and others find an efficient way to inherit someone else's mistakes.

It is also properly multinational, with a wide crypto selection alongside stocks. The cost shows up in **spreads** rather than commissions, and on some instruments those spreads are wider than a US-only competitor would charge.

## Webull - best charting without a subscription

Webull gives away what most platforms charge for: multi-timeframe charts, a deep indicator library, drawing tools, screeners, paper trading and extended-hours data, all in the free tier. For a technical trader who does not want a platform subscription, it is the best value in the category.

Its weakness is education. Webull assumes you know what you are doing, so a newer investor can end up with institutional tooling and no framework for using it.

## Public - best for transparency

Public's pitch answers the routing objection directly: it does not take payment for order flow on equities, funding itself through a paid tier and other revenue lines instead. If that argument is your reason for leaving, this is the platform built for your reason.

It is a smaller product: the range of instruments is narrower and the research is lighter than a legacy broker's.

## M1 Finance - best for automated portfolios

M1 organises holdings into **pies**: you set target percentages, and every deposit is allocated to pull the portfolio back towards those targets. It is a good design for anyone who wants a fixed allocation maintained without doing the arithmetic, and it supports retirement accounts and fractional shares.

It is explicitly not a trading platform. Orders execute in scheduled windows rather than on demand, so if you want to buy at this moment at this price, M1 is the wrong tool. That is a deliberate choice rather than a gap.

## Wealthfront - best for hands-off investing

Wealthfront is a robo-advisor rather than a brokerage you trade in. You answer questions about risk and horizon, it builds a diversified portfolio, then rebalances and runs automated **tax-loss harvesting** on your behalf. In a taxable account held for years, that harvesting can be worth more than the fee.

The fee is the trade-off: an annual management charge on assets, typically around **0.25%**, on top of underlying fund costs. You also give up stock picking. [Betterment](/review/betterment) is the obvious comparison, and the choice usually comes down to account features rather than performance.

## Trading 212 - best for UK and European investors

Robinhood's reach outside the United States is still limited, and Trading 212 fills that space with commission-free stock and ETF investing, fractional shares and, in the UK, a stocks and shares ISA. It is not available to US residents. UK readers should also look at [Freetrade](/review/freetrade), and our guide to the [best investing apps in the UK](/blog/best-investing-apps-uk-2026) covers the tax wrappers in detail.

## How to choose

Start with how often you intend to trade, because it eliminates most of the list immediately. Active traders need execution and charts, which points to Webull or eToro. Long-term holders need low cost and automation, which points to M1 or Wealthfront, and for them the ability to place a fast order is close to irrelevant.

Then ask how much research you want to do yourself. Wanting to be told what to hold is a legitimate answer, and it is what a robo-advisor sells.

Last, check availability and account types first, since geography settles the question more often than features do. A US investor who needs a Roth IRA and a UK investor who needs an ISA are not choosing from the same shortlist.

## The bottom line

Robinhood remains a reasonable choice for what it was built to do: commission-free trading of US stocks and ETFs in a clean app, with no minimum to get started. If that describes your use, switching for its own sake buys you very little.

The case for moving is specific rather than general. Move to Webull or eToro if you have outgrown the tooling, to Public if payment for order flow is a line you do not want to be on the wrong side of, to M1 or Wealthfront if you would rather automate a portfolio than manage one, and to Trading 212 if you are investing from outside the United States. Pick the one whose strength matches the thing Robinhood is not doing for you, and ignore the rest.
